Start With Your Toss — It Controls Everything

If your serve feels off, there's a really good chance your toss is the culprit. A bad toss makes everything downstream harder — your swing path gets awkward, your timing falls apart, and you start muscling the ball instead of swinging through it cleanly.

Here's a simple drill to check your toss right now. Stand in your serving stance, hold your tossing arm straight out in front of you at shoulder height, and let the ball go. Don't flick your wrist. Just open your fingers and release. The ball should land a few inches in front of your front foot and just slightly to your hitting side.

If it's falling behind your head, you're going to struggle to get any real drive on the ball. If it's drifting too far left (for right-handers), you'll be reaching across your body instead of swinging up through the contact point.

Practice the toss motion separately — without even hitting the ball — before your next session. Ten clean tosses in a row before you pick up your racket is a solid warm-up habit.

Check Your Grip Before You Change Anything Else

A lot of players in Nebraska learning the serve on their own default to an Eastern forehand grip because it feels comfortable and like you can "see" the ball better at contact. The problem is that grip basically forces you to hit flat and low, which limits your spin and your consistency on the second serve.

For a more functional serve, try shifting toward a Continental grip. It's the grip where the base knuckle of your index finger sits on the upper bevel of the racket handle — think of it like holding a hammer. It feels awkward at first, and your pace might actually drop for a week or two while you're adjusting. That's completely normal.

The Continental grip unlocks topspin and slice on your serve, which is what gives you a reliable second serve you can actually trust in a match.

Use Your Legs — This Is Free Power

A lot of club players put almost all the work on their arm and shoulder when they serve. That's tiring and it's leaving easy power on the table. Your legs and your hip rotation are where real serve velocity comes from.

Try this: bend your knees more than feels natural during your trophy position — the moment just before you start your upswing. As you swing up to the ball, drive up through your legs and let that energy transfer through your core and shoulder into the racket. You should actually feel your back foot come off the ground naturally if you're driving up correctly.

This doesn't require you to be athletic or strong. It just requires you to stop standing flat-footed while you swing.

The Second Serve Is a Separate Skill — Treat It That Way

Most recreational players treat the second serve like a softer version of the first serve. That's why so many double faults happen. Your second serve should be a different shot entirely — one built on topspin or kick, not just reduced pace.

A basic topspin second serve means brushing up the back of the ball from roughly 7 o'clock to 1 o'clock during your swing. The ball should dip into the service box and bounce higher than a flat serve. It takes repetition to groove, but once it clicks, you'll stop dreading the second ball.

This is genuinely one of the harder things to learn on your own, because the swing path feels counterintuitive. It's also exactly the kind of thing that's much faster to fix in person.
